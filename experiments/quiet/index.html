01 / ARRIVE

A little less
in the foreground.

Choose a texture and press Play. Let it sit at a level where the room still feels present. There is no tune to catch, no next beat to anticipate.

The noise textures are different kinds of randomness. Pink has more air. Brown has more weight. The soft tones offer a small harmonic centre instead.

Move a little way down the page, then pause. Listen for the change in colour rather than a new note. Nothing needs to happen quickly.

02 / NOTICE

Movement can
stay small.

Scrolling opens the sound's upper texture. With soft tones, it also blends one harmonic colour into another. A quicker gesture adds a brief brightness that settles when you stop, while keeping loudness restrained.

The binaural example holds 180 Hz in the left ear. Scrolling changes the right tone smoothly, with a separation of 6–12 Hz, and opens the pink-noise bed beneath it.

Turn Scroll response off to compare the same sound without movement. Silence is a useful comparison too: Pause stops every layer.

03 / STAY

No destination
to reach.

Stop here for a moment. The current texture stays with you; its small response to movement settles without playing more notes.

Try another sound at the same volume. A lower or smoother sound is not automatically more useful. Notice whether it leaves room for what you want to do.

You can keep listening with this tab in the background. Returning up the page traces the same range of colours.

What does the research actually say?

A 2024 review found a small benefit from white/pink noise on laboratory tasks in young people with ADHD or elevated attention difficulties. It does not establish a benefit for everyone, and found no brown-noise studies. Read the noise review ↗

Binaural-beat studies report inconsistent brainwave-entrainment findings. The two-tone example demonstrates the sound, without claiming it changes your brain state. Read the binaural-beat review ↗

Music-based interventions have some evidence for stress-related outcomes. This original, scroll-controlled harmony has not been evaluated for those effects. NCCIH: music and health ↗
